Crows

November 2026

When I was a child, I never knew how fast I would become twenty-one. In 2016, near my house on the banks of the river Gomti in Lucknow, India, a graveyard and a cremation ground stood side by side. I biked there every week and sat in the evenings, mesmerized by the vivid scene. Dead bodies were burned on pyres of oak wood as jubilant sunsets painted the sky, too bright for the dejected family members mourning their lost kin amid the flames. Just across the cremation ground lay an under-maintained British cemetery, its graves silent relics of the British occupation (1757 to 1947). This frame lingers in my mind, firing up randomly like a fever dream. It has been ten years since I left Lucknow, and I can only imagine how it has changed.

Now, in 2026, a stark contrast struck me last night. I am exactly 7,918 miles away in East Lansing, Michigan. My life, my country, my food, my people: all transformed. The weather bites, the pavements are laden with snow, and thousands of students shuffle like frozen zombies across the university. While strolling down a street yesterday, I spotted a mob of crows, and their presence felt eerily familiar. It pulled me back to that nostalgic scene: the Indian last-rite grounds teeming with these large black birds. To me, they seemed majestic and clever.

Crows are not mere scavengers, as people assume; their beaks are not strong enough. They wait craftily for other animals to tear into roadkill, exposing the flesh as it decomposes. They even trick other species for a meal; I have seen a video of them distracting a seal to steal its food. Yet they adapt to whatever comes their way: earthworms, fish, fruits, even dog food. They hide treats for later, acting nonchalant. Hardwired by evolution to survive, they thrive through ingenuity.

On a sleepless night of intense existentialism, I reached a quiet conclusion: in this mysterious passage of life, I sympathize with crows.
